# Pitch-deck Grill Me

Use this interview when the user has a concept, offer, initiative, or recommendation but the case for the audience is not ready. The purpose is to reveal the real decision, test whether the value proposition survives the strongest objection, and gather enough evidence to build a credible deck.

## Interview discipline

- Ask one focused question per turn. Wait for the answer, incorporate it, and choose the next question based on what remains uncertain.
- Be extensive in coverage, not in question dumps. Probe consequential gaps until the case is ready; do not make the user complete every category when it does not apply.
- Start from what the user already gave you. Reuse their words and facts. Never ask them to repeat supplied information.
- Ask only questions whose answers could change the argument, claim, sequence, language, economics, design, or call to action.
- If an answer is vague, test it with a concrete example or ask what observable evidence would make it true. Do not accept “better,” “innovative,” “efficient,” “premium,” “scalable,” or “trusted” as self-explanatory proof.
- Challenge the premise respectfully. Do not agree just to be encouraging. If the offer does not beat the realistic alternative on a material point, say so and explore what would make the case credible.
- Do not invent the user's answer, force a preferred solution, turn a pitch into an interrogation, or use manipulative tactics.
- If the user asks for a fast draft or says to stop grilling, honor that and label material assumptions instead of continuing the interview.

## Explore the case in this order

Use the sequence as a map, not a questionnaire to read aloud. Ask the highest-impact unresolved question next.

### 1. The decision and audience

Establish what the audience is expected to approve, buy, fund, adopt, change, or do. Identify the primary decision-maker, anyone who can block the decision, and the context in which they will see the deck. If there are several audiences, ask who the deck must persuade first and what secondary stakeholders need to hear.

**Useful questions:**

- “What exact decision do you want the audience to make after this deck?”
- “Who makes that decision, and whose objection could stop it?”
- “Will they see this live, read it alone, or use it to brief someone else?”

### 2. The audience's current alternative

Find what the audience already does, buys, believes, or can choose instead. Include status quo, DIY, competitors, delay, or no action as applicable. Understand why the current option is attractive to them; do not describe their alternative as foolish just to make the pitch look strong.

**Useful questions:**

- “What will they do if they decline this?”
- “What do they already have that makes this seem unnecessary?”
- “What do they value in the current way of doing it?”

### 3. The friction, cost, or unmet need

Identify the actual pain, opportunity cost, risk, delay, lost capacity, or unmet requirement. Ask who experiences it, how often, and what happens if it is not addressed. Separate the user's interpretation from evidence the audience would recognize.

**Useful questions:**

- “Where does the current process break down in a real case?”
- “Who spends time or money on that gap, and what is the consequence?”
- “What makes this matter now rather than later?”

### 4. The strongest objection

Ask the skeptical audience question in its sharpest fair form. Address the trade-off openly: price, commission share, control, time, risk, adoption effort, incumbent preference, or uncertainty. Do not answer with a feature list. Ask what would change the audience's mind and whether that evidence is available.

**Useful questions:**

- “What would the most skeptical person in the room say?”
- “What do they give up if they say yes?”
- “What proof would make that trade-off worthwhile?”

### 5. The value mechanism and differentiation

Trace how the offer causes a benefit. Identify the specific capability, process, access, expertise, data, or change that creates it; distinguish that mechanism from a benefit claim. Compare it with the real alternative. Ask what remains useful after an early win and whether advantages compound, scale, or repeat.

**Useful questions:**

- “What changes in the audience's work after they adopt this?”
- “Which step, asset, or capability actually produces the benefit?”
- “Why cannot the audience get the same result from its existing team or partners?”
- “Does value grow with each use, or is this mainly a one-off benefit?”

### 6. Proof and boundaries

Find source documents, operating data, examples, approvals, benchmarks, or customer evidence. Classify each claim as documented fact, user-provided premise, inference, proposal, or illustration. Ask what cannot be claimed yet. For missing proof, choose honest framing or propose a test; never manufacture credibility.

**Useful questions:**

- “What evidence can we show today, and where does it come from?”
- “Which part is confirmed policy, and which part is still a proposal?”
- “What result is not yet proven?”
- “Could a small pilot, baseline, or threshold validate the claim?”

### 7. Economics, effort, and operating reality

When material, clarify price, commission or fee split, net/gross basis, collected/contracted basis, target credit, conversion assumptions, time period, volume, and who performs each step. Compare like with like. Look for the threshold at which the audience breaks even or wins. Include implementation effort, capacity, accountability, and risks that could defeat the promised value.

**Useful questions:**

- “What is the denominator and time period for that number?”
- “Is this amount gross or net, contracted or collected, and how is credit attributed?”
- “What extra volume or saved time is needed to offset the cost?”
- “Who owns the work after approval, and what capacity does it require?”
- “Which assumption would most change the calculation?”

### 8. Voice, terminology, and trust

Use the premium, consultative default in [voice-and-jargon.md](voice-and-jargon.md), then adapt to the audience and the user's preference. Ask about the audience's familiarity with specialist terms only when it affects comprehension or credibility. Capture preferred phrases, terms to avoid, level of formality, and any existing voice sample. Preserve exact contractual or financial distinctions even when simplifying language.

**Useful questions:**

- “Should this sound more like a measured executive recommendation or a direct commercial challenge?”
- “Which terms will this audience understand, and which should we translate?”
- “Are there words or claims your organization avoids?”

### 9. The ask and success condition

Specify the next action, owner, timing, and any low-risk first step. Define what success looks like and how the audience will know the decision worked. If there is no meaningful next action, confirm whether the deck is meant to inform rather than persuade.

**Useful questions:**

- “What should they authorize or do next, and who owns that step?”
- “What would a successful first 30, 60, or 90 days look like?”
- “What evidence would make you stop, change course, or scale it?”

### 10. Delivery constraints

Confirm output type, dimensions or slide count only if important, whether the deck is presented or read, source files, template or brand, speaker notes, deadline, accessibility needs, and any legal or confidentiality boundaries. Prefer user-provided templates and source material over recreating known assets.

## Readiness check

The brief is ready when you can state, in plain language:

1. Who must decide what.
2. What they would do otherwise and why that option appeals.
3. What audience-level problem or opportunity matters.
4. How the offer changes the outcome, specifically.
5. Why this is a better choice despite its cost or trade-off.
6. What proves the case, what is assumed, and what remains unknown.
7. The action to take next and how success will be judged.

If a missing answer could reverse the recommendation or materially change the economics, ask one more question. If it would not change the deck, proceed and avoid burdening the user.

## Close the grill

Before drafting, give a compact **Pitch brief check** with the audience, decision, alternative, core argument, objection, evidence, assumptions, tone, jargon policy, format, and next action. Mark any unresolved point. If a core premise is weak, say so before building and explain the least speculative way to test or qualify it. Then create the deck; do not stop at an interview summary when the user asked for the artifact.
