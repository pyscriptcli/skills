# Anti-Slop Checks for Pitch Decks

Use these checks to make the story and visuals specific, not merely polished. They adapt anti-slop principles to pitch writing and presentations; they do not require a website-style design audit for a static deck.

## Argument and copy

- Replace generic benefits with the audience, situation, mechanism, and consequence. “Save time” is incomplete until the deck says whose time, which task, and what useful work that time can return to.
- Do not make confidence a substitute for proof. Label user premises, sourced facts, inferences, proposals, and examples distinctly.
- Avoid slide titles that only announce a section (“Opportunity,” “Solution,” “Benefits”) when a supported finding can carry the headline.
- Do not repeat the same idea in the title, lede, body, callout, and closing line. Let each sentence advance the case.
- Remove template filler: “Today’s changing landscape,” “Now more than ever,” “In conclusion,” “Let’s explore,” “The future is here,” and boilerplate about being passionate, innovative, or customer-centric.
- Never invent testimonials, customer logos, statistics, market sizes, case studies, ROI, delivery capability, approval, or urgency.
- Challenge a feature list that does not connect each capability to an audience outcome. Cut a benefit that cannot be explained or evidenced.
- Avoid false balance, straw-man objections, and rhetoric that hides cost or shifts blame to the audience.

## Composition and visuals

- Choose a visual form because it clarifies a decision: comparison for trade-offs, process for ownership, chart for evidence, timeline for sequence, map for coverage, or equation for economics.
- Give each slide one focal point and one job. Use whitespace to separate logic.
- Vary layouts where the argument changes. Do not duplicate the same three-card or four-card grid across the whole deck for convenience.
- Use no decoration just to make a page feel finished: icons, stock photos, gradients, shadows, ornamental shapes, dense footers, fake device frames, and gratuitous charts all need a job.
- Prefer supplied, relevant photography and official logos. Never generate a logo, invent a client mark, or imply an endorsement.
- Keep charts honest: show baseline, units, period, source, and assumptions; use scales that do not distort the comparison; do not chart data that can be clearer as a sentence.
- Make meaningful numbers legible and visually secondary data quieter. Do not use color alone to distinguish meaning.

## Final review

For every slide, write the one-line reason for its layout, visual, and main emphasis. If there is no concrete reason, simplify. Then remove anything that is not evidence, explanation, orientation, or a necessary transition. Check that the deck still makes sense when read in sequence without the speaker's sales patter.
